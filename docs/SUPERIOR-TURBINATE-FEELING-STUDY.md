## 2. WHAT THE CREATOR SAYS SHE IS DOING (paraphrased, short phrases only)

Evangelia Artemis-Gomez, born and raised in New York, mother from Greece, father Jewish Puerto Rican; her mother was a cartoon colourist on Codename: Kids Next Door. Influences she names: Ralph Bakshi, Daria, Downtown, David Firth's Salad Fingers. At 17 she made a comic called "Via" to process being a rowdy teenager; a college After Effects assignment became the first $UPEREGO animation, with music by her girlfriend and bandmate Carmen Caska.

Why Freud, why girls: as a teenager she read Freud and Jung while trying to work out whether she was a good person without talking to adults about it. The angel/devil-on-the-shoulder idea felt played out, so she personified self-regulation as a female friend group — the girls check and validate each other — as a vehicle for moral questions. Via = Id, Blade = Ego, Yara = Superego. Yara's long monologues draw on 90s punk, feminist archives, and outsider art.

The photo backgrounds are pictures she took in Puerto Rico, Greece, and New York, embedding her heritage. Viewers from many places tell her the locations feel like their own hometowns — the grime, the parks, the guys outside the bodega — and she says there is beauty in those overlooked corners, where underground scenes develop before they get co-opted.

The feeling she wants: she describes herself as a teenager who was anxious, depressed, and struggling with substance use, with a hope in her that "wouldn't die." She turned nihilism into absurdism and excitement, and she likes the term "hopepunk": in the show's small moments she wants viewers to see there is something to aspire to — better versions of ourselves and the world exist, we just need to seek them out. The show speaks in the nihilistic language Gen Z understands and then gives way to hope. She also wants to preserve what her family's New York block felt like — relatives who lived there have died and places have been torn down — so the show becomes an everlasting world that can house these emotions.

What it is for (Patreon About, paraphrased): raise enough money to make a full-length episodic series and make the show the creators' day jobs "so you never have to go without the girls." Perks: early episodes, Yara advice videos, AMA live streams, music breakdowns, BTS process videos, concept art, merch discount codes, Discord access. Uploads weekly (early episodes + Yara advice + BTS), monthly AMAs. No merch exists yet — a discount comes "once our merch is live."

Her father Jeff Gomez posts about the show on LinkedIn, calling out its joke density and timing and saying it "sneaks up on you" with questions.

## 3. THE FOUR-PART FEELING MACHINE

### 3a. Recognition — three girls who ARE your own mind

The characters are not just funny — they are the parts of the viewer's own head, drawn from the creator's own adolescence, speaking in the exact register of group-chat philosophy, substance jokes, and moral panic. The viewer recognizes herself in them before she laughs at them. That is why "literally me" and "relatable" and "representation" show up 61 times, and why a mother writes that the show is about healing and not repeating patterns. The show does not observe its audience from above. It IS its audience, with a camera on.

### 3b. The lecture as love — Yara's monologue is the payload

Yara's monologues are the product. Long, earnest, slightly unhinged essays on the soul, corporations, child development — delivered inside a 2-minute joke cartoon, so the medicine arrives as a punchline. The structure is always the same: joke, joke, joke, then Yara gets sincere for 20–30 seconds, then the joke resumes or the episode hard-cuts. The sincerity is never announced and never apologized for. It lands because the strictest friend in the group is the one saying it — the lecture IS the love. The 1,300-like comment is not about a joke. It is about feeling comforted every time she talks.

### 3c. Music as the hook and the afterlife — the show is as much a band as a cartoon

Carmen Caska writes one original song per episode. The songs are the emotional hook: episode 6 opens with the full song — "Genius Penis Girl" — playing for roughly its first 25 seconds over the long driving shot (a pink Toyota Avalon on a photo highway) before any dialogue. Every episode clip on TikTok uses a Carmen original as its sound (Ep.1 "Mad Tea Party (Theme)", Ep.2 "BLUE LIGHT POISONING", Ep.3 "VHS (Part Machine)", Ep.4 "Test Loop", Ep.5 "Yara's Monologue", Ep.6 "Genius Penis Girl", Ep.8 "Ghost Town", Ep.9 "Girls' Theme (Trauma Rangoons)", Ep.10 "Yara's Theme"). The non-episode clips use other artists' audio instead — the Carmen songs are reserved for the episodes themselves. They are the show's signature sound, not background filler.

Then the songs live on: two are released as singles on Spotify via DistroKid, under Carmen Caska (©/℗ Animal Angel, her band) — "Genius Penis Girl (from $uperego ost)", 1:31, released Oct 9, 2026, 32,781 Spotify plays at checking; and "Broke Hoe (from $uperego)", 2:20, released Nov 20, 2026, the same day Ep.10 hit YouTube. The other episode songs exist as TikTok sounds and Patreon OST posts but have no Spotify release yet — only two of ten are streamable. Commenters keep begging for the rest. Fan-made Spotify playlists titled "$UPEREGO" exist. One platform quirk worth knowing: searching TikTok for "genius penis girl" returns "No results found" — the title itself is unsearchable there, so the song circulates only through the episode clip.

The rule, stated plainly: **one original song per episode, the song opens the episode before the dialogue, the song is released as a single the same day the episode drops.** The music is not decoration. It is how the feeling survives after the episode ends — in a playlist, in a TikTok sound, hummed later.

### 3d. The parasocial loop — the character becomes a real person in the audience's life

**Yara's Advice Corner.** Fans submit real personal issues through a Google Form; every week, the submission that interests Yara most gets an answer in a video of Yara advising — an extra weekly video on top of the episode ($10 "EGO" tier and up). Two numbered advice videos are posted so far, both locked, and their titles alone show the range of what fans submit: "1 - Should I get a Spiteful BBL?" (Nov 11) and "2 - Scared to do shrooms with my slime-loving gf" — body-image/cosmetic-surgery decisions and drug/relationship anxiety, the same confessional, slightly unhinged territory as the episodes. The format's role is exact: Yara, the Superego, becomes a literal advice columnist — the character who lectures in the episodes now lectures the audience about their own lives, turning a fictional moral voice into a real parasocial relationship. A fan who submitted a problem and got Yara's answer is not a viewer anymore. She is a participant.

**The Discord and the fan art.** The server is small — 90 members, 20 online at checking, co-run by Carmen (handle princessroach / carmencaska9). The Ep.9 YouTube description thanks fans for "hopping on the discord and the patreon and sending in fan art and halloween costumes" — fan art and Halloween costumes are an acknowledged, solicited part of the community. On TikTok the top fan video is @tavi_wavie's 17-second animation meme of Via dancing with a red lollipop — 49.1K likes, 123 comments, 6,548 saves — using the #lps animation-meme trend format, meaning fans are redrawing the girls as meme templates. There is a Yara cosplay (1,040 likes), a "Via mood board bc she's so cool and so me" (386 likes), and fan art of fan art. A 76.8K-like post lists $uperego among favorite artists with the caption "support REAL art!", and TikTok's related searches include "is $uperego ai" — fans are actively defending the show as hand-made against AI suspicion. A fan-made Fandom wiki exists (10 pages, home page still an empty stub). Patreon WELCOME comments show fans who followed from her earlier project ("I was a member of What's Gonna Happen so of course I had to support the next endeavor").

**The loop:** a TikTok clip leads to YouTube, YouTube leads to Patreon, Patreon leads to Discord, and the Discord sends fan art back into the show's world. Each piece feeds the next. And the honest limit is the point: this is a two-person operation with a 90-person Discord and 340 paying patrons, so the "world" is still small — which is precisely why it feels intimate. Smallness is not a weakness of the feeling machine. It IS the feeling machine.

### 3e. Hopepunk — the thesis underneath it all

Nihilism, spoken in the language the audience already speaks, and then giving way to hope — in small moments, not in a speech. Better versions of ourselves and the world exist, we just need to seek them out. And the show itself is the preservation: an everlasting world that can house these emotions, holding a block and a family that are gone in real life. The show is not just a cartoon. It is a place that stays.

## 4. THE CAPE JONES FEELING BLUEPRINT — the same feeling, in our languages

The feeling to build is the one from §1: **understood by someone exactly as messed up as you are, then told — by the strictest friend in the group — that it is going to be okay.** Cape Jones does not have a Yara, a Via, and a Blade. It does not need them. It has its own languages, and each one already carries a part of the machine. The mapping below is the blueprint. Nothing here is a build order — it is the feeling spec the builds must hit.

### 4a. Recognition — Jones IS the audience's messed-up part

$UPEREGO's recognition comes from three girls who are the parts of one mind. Cape Jones's recognition comes from one guy who is the gap between who he thinks he is and who he is: a washed-up cosmic hero who keeps "saving" a town that never asked, fails upward, and gets credit anyway. Every viewer who has ever talked big and delivered small recognizes him instantly — the same way $UPEREGO's viewers recognize themselves in the girls. The bible's flaw engine (wants glory → attempts feat → succeeds at the wrong thing / fails at the right thing → gets credit anyway → tag undercuts him) IS the recognition engine. It must never be softened into him actually winning, and never be sharpened into him being humiliated. The feeling lives in the middle: he fails, it is funny, and he is still standing there, still Jones.

**The universe languages are the voices in his head.** The JoJo-derived universe languages the owner named are Cape Jones's version of the Id/Ego/Superego trio — not three characters, but three registers inside one guy: the cosmic register (what he thinks he is), the mundane register (what he actually is), and the cape's register (what he should be). When the short cuts between those registers, the audience hears its own head arguing with itself. That is the $UPEREGO move, done in our language.

### 4b. The lecture as love — the sincere beat inside the joke

$UPEREGO's payload is Yara's monologue. Cape Jones's payload is the sincere beat the bible already writes: one true line, inside the gag, never announced. "Ah, that's better." is the whole Yara monologue compressed into four words — a guy who fails at everything, alone in a parking lot at night, fixes the light and is quietly satisfied. The parking-lot short (the proposed test short from Part 2) is the first feeling test for exactly this reason: the lighting shift IS the lecture. The room changes color when he feels something. The audience feels comforted not because Jones lectures them, but because the world gets better for one second and he notices.

**Who delivers the love in Cape Jones:** the two characters who take him seriously when nobody else does.

- **The Cape.** The bible names the Cape as the most competent character in the show. The cape-got-there-first gag is not just a gag — it is the moral voice acting. The Cape does the competent thing while Jones does nothing. That is Yara, worn on his back: the superego, literally. When the Cape acts and Jones watches, the audience gets the same feeling $UPEREGO gives when Yara speaks — somebody stricter and better than me is looking out for me.
- **The Fan.** The Fan kid is the audience surrogate who takes Jones seriously. In $UPEREGO the audience's love flows toward Yara; in Cape Jones it flows through the Fan — a kid who believes in the hero, which gives the audience permission to believe in him too. The Fan's sincerity is the show's entry point for the feeling. Protect it. The Fan never mocks Jones. The town mocks Jones. The Fan does not.

**The timing rule, carried from Part 2 and tightened here:** the punchline is line + beat of silence + held reaction, no sting, no music bed under the dialogue. In Cape Jones the held reaction must be DRAWN, not frozen — a pose change, arms down, the cape settling, the light shifting. Motion in the stillness. That is how the sincere beat lands at our animation bar instead of becoming the slideshow the owner already rejected.

### 4c. Music as the hook and the afterlife — the Metalocalypse layer, and Real is the Carmen slot

$UPEREGO's rule: one original song per episode, the song opens the episode before the dialogue, the song is released as a single the same day. Cape Jones's version of this is the Metalocalypse layer — the show's music is not background, it is one of the three genres the show is made of. And the mapping is direct: **Real makes the music, the way Carmen makes $UPEREGO's.** The dance song already exists (cj-dance-song-v2, song-full-v2-jp.mp3 on release `cj-dance-song-v2-20261115`) — that is the seed. The rule for the shorts: one original track per short, the track opens the short, the track drops as a single the same day the short drops. The parking-lot short gets its own track (or its own cut of the dance song). What is a GAP, stated plainly: there are no per-episode originals yet and no streaming releases yet. The music machine does not exist until the first single ships alongside the first short. Do not claim it before then.

### 4d. The parasocial loop — "Ask Cape Jones", the water tower, and fan art from day one

**"Ask Cape Jones" is the Advice Corner, in our language.** The Fan brings real fan questions to Jones, Jones answers them wrong-but-sincere, and the Cape corrects him. Same format as Yara's Advice Corner (real issues, one answer a week, the character advising the audience about their own lives), but the comedy comes from Jones being the WORST advice-giver in the world and the Cape being the real advisor — the audience gets the parasocial relationship AND the show's core gag in the same video. This needs the owner's voice (Jones answers) — it is a recording ask, not a build. Do not fake it.

**The water tower is the Discord.** $UPEREGO's Discord is 90 people and that smallness is the intimacy. Cape Jones's HQ is a decommissioned water tower — the show's world already has a hangout. The Discord, when it opens, is the water tower: the place fans gather, the place fan art gets posted, the place the show's world lives between episodes. Evangelia's line about the show being an everlasting world that can house these emotions maps directly onto the tower and the town — a preserved place that feels like your hometown. $UPEREGO's photo backgrounds work because they are real photos from Puerto Rico, Greece, and New York, and viewers say the locations feel like their own hometowns. Cape Jones's environments should do the same trick with Real's world: the parking lot, the water tower, the couch — real places, drawn true, so viewers say "that looks like my town."

**Fan art and cosplay, solicited from day one.** $UPEREGO's Ep.9 description thanks fans for fan art and Halloween costumes. Cape Jones should do the same in its descriptions from the first short: thank the fan art, name the cosplay, repost the redraws. The audience must see, from episode one, that the show's world has room for them in it. The #lps meme-template fan video (49.1K likes) is the proof that fans redrawing the characters is a growth engine, not a side effect — give them a character simple enough to redraw (Jones's locked palette and silhouette are already that: white suit, purple cowl, black cape) and a gag simple enough to meme (the remote).

### 4e. Hopepunk, in Cape Jones words

$UPEREGO's thesis: nihilism in the audience's own language, giving way to hope in small moments. Cape Jones's thesis is the same shape, in the show's own words: **the world is a dim orange streetlight, he fixes the light, it's better. Not saved — better.** Jones is a cosmic hero who fails upward. The hope is not that he wins. The hope is that he keeps showing up and the town keeps letting him. The mundane bullshit IS the hope: doing nothing, together, in a lit parking lot, is enough. That is the feeling the parking-lot short has to produce in 30 seconds. If the viewer walks away thinking "ah, that's better," the short worked — whether or not they can say why.

### 4f. Smallness on purpose

$UPEREGO is two people, 340 paying patrons, a 90-person Discord — and that smallness is why it feels intimate. Cape Jones should launch small on purpose, not as a limitation to apologize for: the parking-lot short goes to TikTok first (the funnel), YouTube for the binge, Patreon for early episodes + the Fan's questions + BTS, Discord as the water tower. Do not chase Adult Swim from day one. Let the audience beg for it — the way $UPEREGO's comments beg for Adult Swim 34 times in five episodes. The begging is the proof the feeling landed.

## 5. WHAT WE COPY, WHAT WE DON'T, WHAT'S STILL A GAP

**Copy:** the packaging, the writing shape (cold open, one location, one sincere beat, hard-cut ending), the funnel (TikTok finds them, YouTube is where they binge), the every-other-Friday cadence, the one-original-song-per-episode rule, the advice format, the fan-art solicitation, the smallness-on-purpose launch.

**Don't copy:** the long holds (15–30 seconds on one shot) — the owner already rejected that for Cape Jones, and the feeling does not require holds; the sincere beat lands in Cape Jones through a drawn pose change and a lighting shift, not a freeze. Don't copy the three-girl structure — our voices-in-his-head are the universe languages, the Cape, and the Fan.

**Gaps, stated plainly:** no per-short originals released yet and no streaming singles yet (the music machine starts when the first single ships with the first short); "Ask Cape Jones" needs the owner's recorded voice; the Discord/water tower is not open yet; no merch yet (same as $UPEREGO — they have none either, and their fans still beg for it); Instagram is unverified (login-walled, same as Part 2); the Advice Corner videos and music tutorials are paywalled, so their contents are known from titles only.

**The first feeling test stays what Part 2 proposed:** THE PARKING LOT LED remote scene. One location, one gag, one big lighting shift, one sincere line. If it makes a viewer feel "ah, that's better," the blueprint works. Proposal only — the build is a separate decision, and it is running now as its own lane.
