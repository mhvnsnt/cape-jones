## 1. METALOCALYPSE — the Flash vector puppet rig

**The show:** created by Brendon Small and Tommy Blacha for Adult Swim; animated by Titmouse, Inc. + Williams Street; 4 seasons, 2006–2013, 11-minute episodes, 24fps broadcast; film *Metalocalypse: Army of the Doomstar* (2023). About the death-metal band Dethklok, the seventh-largest economy on Earth.

### 1a. How it is actually made — draw once, rig, pose, tween

The production pipeline, in the artist's own description (from a listing of original Metalocalypse production drawings by lead character designer Songgu Kwon):

1. Rough pencil sketch of the figure.
2. Clean line version drawn on a light table.
3. The clean drawing is **scanned and recreated as vector line art in Flash**.
4. The vector version is **colored and rigged (like a puppet)**.
5. Animators pose and tween the rig — that is the animation.

The consequence the listing states plainly: unlike old-fashioned hand-drawn cel animation, where a few minutes of footage might require hundreds of drawings, Metalocalypse has **relatively few physical drawings of characters, since the animation was done digitally using computer software**. Some peripheral characters were created by "Frankensteining" various body parts together and **only exist in digital form** — they were never drawn as whole characters on paper at all.

That is the whole secret of the show's look: the drawings are few, the rigs are everything, and the motion comes from posing the rigs — not from redrawing the characters.

### 1b. Budget discipline — the number that made the method

Brendon Small benchmarked the cheapest Adult Swim show he could find — *Tom Goes to the Mayor*, $110,000 per episode — and asked Titmouse to do Metalocalypse for under that. Titmouse agreed to **$109,099 per episode averaged over the season** (Wikipedia). The puppet-rig method is not a style choice on top of a big budget. It IS the budget: draw once, rig, reuse forever.

### 1c. Music — story first, music second, and the music must stand alone

- Small's rule for the songs (podcast interview, transcribed by Killer Guitar Rigs): early on he had a small bank of songs and matched their energy to episode topics. Once that bank ran out, **he had the story first, and then did the music second** — writing songs to serve each episode's mood and feeling.
- The writing is fast and iterative: about two hours for the parts of a song, lyrics a month or two later, revisit a year or two later and finish it. In the studio with drummer Gene Hoglan, **one or two finished songs a day** — scratch track, Gene learns it on the spot, seven or eight passes, cull the best pieces (Modern Drummer, 2010).
- The bar Small set for himself: "If we can make this music cool enough to stand on its own, I think we're gonna make this work."
- The animation is **carefully synced to the music, with the chord positions and fingering of the guitar parts shown in some detail** (Wikipedia). The rig has to be able to play the instrument correctly — the puppet work serves the music, not the other way around.

### 1d. Format discipline — 11 minutes, cut hard

- Chris Prynoski (Titmouse co-founder, directed seasons 1–2) in a MediaMikes interview: the hardest challenge was fitting everything into the 11-minute format — Small and Blacha are improv comedians, and episodes came back with **hours of material** that had to be cut down in the edit and still be funny and satisfying.
- Season 3 tried 22-minute episodes. Season 4 went back to 11 minutes, because "that's the format that works."

### 1e. Where Titmouse came from

Titmouse started as a t-shirt company that used short Flash animations to promote its designs; the animation was the standout part, so the Prynoskis restructured into an animation studio (LBB Online, Shannon Prynoski interview). Metalocalypse was the first TV series Shannon Prynoski produced. The Flash-puppet method is the studio's founding skill, not a later optimisation.

### 1f. What Cape Jones takes from Metalocalypse

- **COPY:** draw once → vector → rig like a puppet → pose/tween the rig. The rig is the show. Few drawings, many poses.
- **COPY:** the budget logic — the rig is what makes a small team afford TV-level motion.
- **COPY:** 11-minute format for episodes; cut hard in the edit; if a 22-minute cut doesn't work, go back to 11.
- **COPY:** story first, music second — the song serves the episode's feeling. And the music must stand on its own (the Dethalbum rule: bits of songs in the show get expanded into full tracks).
- **COPY:** animation synced to the music when music plays — the puppet has to actually play it.
- **DO NOT COPY:** the metal subject matter, the gore, the band premise. Cape Jones has its own world, cast, and gags. Import the method, not the content.

## 2. AQUA TEEN HUNGER FORCE — the Photoshop + After Effects puppet

**The show:** created by Dave Willis and Matt Maiellaro for Adult Swim (Williams Street); first aired 2001; the longest-running Adult Swim series; a Space Ghost Coast to Coast spin-off about three fast-food roommates (Master Shake, Frylock, Meatwad) and their neighbour Carl. Seasons 1–11 (2001–2015), season 12 in 2023.

### 2a. How it is actually made, seasons 1–11 — draw, scan, cut apart, puppet

Per Wikipedia's production section and a 2005 Creative Loafing cover story ("a cartoon is born"):

1. Characters and backgrounds are **drawn on paper, then scanned into Adobe Photoshop where they are colored. But that's where the drawing ended.**
2. Every element of the show — Meatwad, a chair, a fry — **exists as a Photoshop or QuickTime file**.
3. Those files are **composited and animated in After Effects**, and assembled in Final Cut Pro.
4. The process relies on **reusing static digital graphics and limited cutout movements** rather than full keyframe animation.

The budget: a script is cranked out in **two or three days**; ten to twelve weeks later the 11-minute episode is finished for **$75,000–$200,000**. Switching from Avid to Apple + Final Cut Pro saved the production 75 percent of its budget and two weeks per episode.

The recycling discipline, stated by editor/producer Jay Edwards: they manage a library of images and **recycle what they can to keep costs low**. "That's why Meatwad only morphs into a hot dog and igloo, 'cause that's all we could afford to do." New elements get added; old ones get reused. A transformation that can't be afforded becomes a cutaway and a cut back.

### 2b. The Awesome Inc. years — how the puppet actually moves

The last three seasons were produced by creative studio Awesome Inc. (2012–2015). In a 2015 Animation World Network interview, producer Brandon Betts, lead compositor/VFX artist Nate Churney, and founder Ashley Kohler describe the process:

- **Team of five or six people** — "maybe five or six, all told" — versus forty to fifty animators and compositors on bigger shows.
- **Radio play first.** Episodes start at Williams Street with the writers and producers. They cut the radio play and animatics, then hand the studio "sort of like a loose blueprint."
- **No storyboarding phase.** "Unlike other shows, *Aqua Teen* doesn't really go through a storyboarding phase. It's more like episodes are directly blocked out in edit." The animatic is bare-bones — it just implies the action — and the studio fleshes it out, "throwing little things in here and there."
- **The puppeteer.** Nate Churney does "a hybrid style of animation. He gets a lot with a little. A lot of times, characters won't necessarily be fully animated in the traditional sense. Nate will kind of puppeteer them." Full animation and puppeted movement are blended shot by shot — the team decides when to fully animate and when to puppet.
- **Cheats that read as style.** "Instead of fully animating a walk cycle Nate will kind of popsicle stick the character in." When two gestures have no tween between them, "I've got to figure out which arms I can use and chop up in After Effects to get that tween looking right."
- **Creative recycling as a craft.** Carl has **five or six arm moves that you may see the entire series** — and Churney gets "a lot of different emotions out of the same arm movements by playing with timing and speed of the animation."
- **Tools:** paper → full Photoshop frame-by-frame animation → After Effects, where Churney handles the puppeteer animation, compositing, and effects.
- **Sound is part of the animation.** "It comes a little bit from everyone. Everything from sound design to the editor's pacing and the composite with effects." Michael Kohler handled music and sound design for ten years and wrote the theme song.

### 2c. Season 12 — the method changes

For the twelfth season (2023), production moved to Floyd County Productions and switched to **conventional Flash animation** — more detailed art, conventional digital 2D vector animation (Wikipedia). Same puppet-rig idea as Metalocalypse, one generation newer, with a bigger look.

### 2d. Why it feels alive despite the stiffness

The AWN interview names it directly: "fans will say the show's choppy and limited animation style, coupled with great writing and voice work, is what makes *Aqua Teen* so funny." The aliveness does not come from drawing count. It comes from:

1. **Dialogue first.** The radio play is cut before any animation exists. The show is built on the soundtrack — voices, timing, ad-libs (Willis and Maiellaro voice their own characters and improvise in the booth; ad-libs get added to the script on the spot).
2. **Timing variation on recycled moves.** The same five arm moves read as five different emotions because the timing and speed change.
3. **Motion concentrated where it matters.** Faces, explosions, the gag beat — the puppet moves where the joke is; everything else holds.
4. **Sound design + editor pacing + composite.** The humour is cooked in the edit, not in the drawing count.
5. **A look that welcomes the viewer in.** Simple characters, instantly recognisable — "the look of the show is instantly recognizable and instantly approachable."

### 2e. What Cape Jones takes from Aqua Teen Hunger Force

- **COPY:** draw → scan → cut apart → puppet in the compositor. The ATHF sentence is the CJ sentence: "You take a static image of Frylock, cut it into a few pieces — the goatee, the eyes, the fries — and you rotate them slightly. That's it. That is the whole show."
- **COPY:** radio play / dialogue first, animatic as a loose blueprint, episodes blocked out in edit. The voice performance leads; the puppet follows.
- **COPY:** the recycling discipline — a small library of arm moves, reused with different timing and speed for different emotions. The rig library is an asset, not a shortcut to hide.
- **COPY:** the hybrid rule — decide shot by shot when to fully animate and when to puppet. Puppet is the default; full animation is rationed to the beats that need it.
- **COPY:** cheats that read as style — popsicle-stick the character in, chop up the arm in the compositor, cut away and cut back when a move can't be afforded. Never pretend the cheat is full animation; make the cheat funny.
- **COPY:** the small team. Five or six people can make a show that runs fifteen years if the pipeline is honest about what it is.
- **DO NOT COPY:** the crude look as an excuse. ATHF's stiffness is a chosen style with a specific joke engine behind it — Cape Jones's bar is comic-book defined faces and the locked palette, and the owner already rejected flat/cartoony. The method transfers; the crudeness does not.

### 3c. Timing — an honest limit on what subtitles can measure

The transcript data proves **dialogue density**, not hold lengths, and this lane states that plainly:

- Ep8 auto-captions: 135 cues over 124.2 seconds, **100% coverage, zero gaps over 0.5s**. Auto-captions tile the timeline continuously — they cannot measure silence or holds.
- Ep9 SRT: 69 cues, spoken time 233.6s over a 120.8s span (**193% coverage**) — overlapping speaker cues. One gap over 0.5s in the whole episode (1.7s).
- Ep10 SRT: 78 cues, 263.9s spoken over 139.1s (**190% coverage**). Two gaps over 0.5s; longest 2.7s.

What this proves: the dialogue is near-constant and overlapping — the girls talk over each other, and there is almost never a silent gap in the soundtrack. What it does NOT prove: how long any visual shot holds. **Visual hold lengths for eps 8/9/10 stay unwatched** — no timed re-watch was done in this lane, and a subtitle file cannot substitute for one. The timed numbers from Part 2 stand (ep7: holds 10–25s; ep6: holds 25–30s) and are NOT extended to eps 8–10 here.

### 3e. What Cape Jones takes from $UPEREGO (updated from Parts 1–3)

- **COPY:** the packaging — cold open, one location, one sincere beat + one undercut, hard-cut ending landing the title phrase or a button line last, meme-bait two-word titles, no subscribe-begging, same hashtags from day one.
- **COPY:** the funnel — TikTok as discovery, YouTube as the binge home, Patreon a week early, biweekly Fridays.
- **COPY:** one original song per short, the song opens the short, the song drops as a single the same day. (The $UPEREGO lag — 2 of 10 streamable — is the mistake to avoid, not the model.)
- **COPY:** the sincere beat as the shareable engine — but in Cape Jones the beat lands through a drawn pose change and a lighting shift, never a freeze.
- **COPY:** the advice format ("Ask Cape Jones") and the fan-art solicitation from day one.

- **DO NOT COPY:** the photobash look as the only look, the teen drug/sex/taboo content, or the school setting. The main show keeps its own look; photobash stays a test lane at most.

## 4. THE THREE METHODS SIDE BY SIDE

| | Metalocalypse | Aqua Teen Hunger Force | $UPEREGO |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Studio** | Titmouse, Inc. + Williams Street | Williams Street → Awesome Inc. | 2 people (Evangelia + Carmen) |
| **Team** | Full TV crew | 5–6 people (Awesome Inc.) | 2 |
| **Drawn art** | Draw once, vector in Flash, rig | Draw on paper, scan, colour in Photoshop — then drawing ends | Flat 2D girls drawn once, photobash backgrounds |
| **Motion** | Rig posed + tweened (Flash puppet) | Cut-apart puppet in After Effects; full animation blended in shot by shot | Held poses, mouth flaps, head bobs; hero shot rationed to the punchline |
| **Audio first?** | Story first, music second; animation synced to the playing | Radio play cut first; dialogue leads; ad-libs in the booth | Dialogue first; one original song per episode opens it |
| **Format** | 11 min, 24fps | 11 min, 24fps | 1:06–2:28, biweekly Fridays |
| **Budget** | ~$109,099 / ep | $75K–$200K / ep | ~$0 + Patreon (1.1K members, 340 paid) |
| **Reuse** | Rig reused forever; peripheral characters Frankensteined from parts | Library of images; Carl's 5–6 arm moves reused all series with different timing | Same trio, same argument, new costume every episode |
| **Ending** | Hard cut / song button | Hard cut | Hard cut, title phrase or button line last |
| **What makes it feel alive** | The rig moves every frame; the music is real and synced | Dialogue density, timing variation on recycled moves, sound + edit | The sincere monologue + the feeling of being understood |

The shared truth across all three: **the drawing count is never the aliveness.** Metalocalypse has few physical drawings. ATHF has five arm moves for Carl. $UPEREGO has held poses. What makes all three feel like TV is that **something is always moving on purpose** — a rig posing, a puppet's arm chopped into a new timing, a mouth flapping on dense dialogue, a light changing colour — and the edit never lets a dead frame sit.

## 5. WHAT CAPE JONES TAKES — THE COMBINED RULE

1. **Rig-first, always.** Draw once, rig like a puppet, pose the rig. That is the Metalocalypse method, and it is now the Cape Jones method (owner order 2026-11-22). A cut with no rig is a slideshow, and the owner has rejected two of those.
2. **Dialogue first.** The radio play / voice line is cut before the animation is posed. The puppet follows the voice, never the other way around. (ATHF + $UPEREGO agree; Cape Jones's voice is the owner's recording only — never synthesized.)
3. **The rig moves every 2 frames.** A new rig pose every 2 frames at 24fps (12 poses/sec). The computer tweens between poses. Nothing ever sits static the way V1's crossfades did. (Metalocalypse's tween + ATHF's chopped-arm timing, at our twos bar.)
4. **Holds are drawn motion, never freezes.** The held reaction after the punchline is a pose change, arms down, the cape settling, the light shifting. Motion in the stillness. (SHOW-PRODUCTION-STATUS §4: "Adult Swim snap-hold timing (short snap, then a hold with life), not a 3-second freeze.")
5. **Real painted backgrounds, never void-white.** The lot, the water tower, the couch — real places, drawn true, so viewers say "that looks like my town." ($UPEREGO's photo backgrounds do the same job with real photos.)
6. **Ration the spectacle.** One hero shot per short — the dynamic colored lighting shift. The rest is posing, dialogue, and timing. (Metalocalypse rations its shred; $UPEREGO rations its one hero shot; ATHF rations full animation.)
7. **Recycle honestly.** The rig library is an asset. The same arm move with different timing and speed is a different emotion. Log the reuse; never hide it; never call a reused pose a new drawing.
8. **One sincere beat + one undercut per short.** The lighting shift IS the lecture in PURPLE LOT. The tag undercuts it. That pairing is what gets shared twice.
9. **Music is a single, not a bed.** One original per short, opens the short, drops as a single the same day. Real is the Carmen slot. No music bed under the line, no temp track.
10. **Hard cut to black.** No fade, no sting. Line + beat of silence + drawn held reaction, then black.
